01
Perception → action

Gaze reveals learning before movement

The question

Implicit motor learning is usually judged by how fast people respond. But could the eyes reveal learning earlier — before the hands catch up?

What we did

Participants performed a reach-grasp-and-move sequence task across five illuminated plates while we tracked anticipatory gaze and response time on every single trial.

What we found

Differences between learners appeared in anticipatory gaze about 70 trials before they showed up in response time — an ordering that held in 95.5% of bootstrap resamples. Anticipatory looks became more frequent with practice, and most people responded faster on trials where they looked ahead.

Why it matters

Gaze could serve as an early, non-invasive marker of who is learning — opening the door to training and rehabilitation that adapts before performance plateaus.

02
Vision & posture

Seeing ahead to stay stable

The question

When you stop a rolling ball or a closing door, your body braces before contact. Do you need to follow the object with your eyes to prepare well?

What we did

Using a robotic interface, participants stopped virtual objects moving toward them while either tracking them with smooth pursuit or fixating centrally or peripherally.

What we found

Postural preparation was weakest with central fixation, but peripheral vision was as effective as pursuit tracking. Earlier work showed that pursuit-related signals tune anticipatory hand force, and that gaze gain and velocity predict how strongly the body prepares.

Why it matters

Vision outside the center of gaze plays a major role in keeping us stable — knowledge that informs balance assessment and training.

03
Wearables

Measuring prosthesis use in daily life

The question

Clinics know how long people wear a prosthesis — not whether they actually use it to do things. How can we measure real, functional use in daily life?

What we did

With engineering collaborators we built a transradial prosthesis simulator with embedded pressure, strain and temperature sensors, and recorded people during structured tasks and everyday activities.

What we found

A random-forest model separated goal-directed use, non-task use and passive wear with 95% precision. A simple variability-only model distinguished ‘use’ from ‘rest’ with an AUC of 0.91 — no calibration needed.

Why it matters

Lightweight, interpretable sensing can give clinicians objective feedback on prosthesis use at home, supporting personalized rehabilitation.

04
Aging

Why older adults learn differently

The question

Older adults are more likely to lose balance when things move around them. Is that because aging affects every visual route equally — or only some?

What we did

Eighteen young and eighteen older adults stopped a virtual object approaching at four speeds while tracking it, fixating centrally, or fixating to the side.

What we found

With pursuit tracking, both groups scaled their bracing equally well. With peripheral vision, older adults' force-rate scaling was significantly weaker — they compensated by starting earlier.

Why it matters

Peripheral motion processing emerges as a specific, potentially trainable source of age-related postural vulnerability.
